Module 02  ·  Vertical resolution

The wedge model, and the thickness that lies to you

A bed thins to nothing. Its top and base reflections move together until they merge into one event that keeps getting brighter — then dims — while the seismic keeps reporting the same thickness no matter how thin the bed actually gets. Every amplitude map you have ever made has this effect baked into it.

Try this

  1. Find the brightest part of the wedge. Drag the marker to the brightest column. Is it at the thick end? Compare the measured tuning thickness in the readout with λ/4. The peak sits near λ/5, not λ/4 — the familiar rule of thumb is about 25% optimistic for a Ricker wavelet. Below that thickness the bed keeps thinning but the seismic gets dimmer, not thinner.
  2. Break the thickness map. Watch the lower plot as you drag left from the thick end. Where does the red curve peel away from the dashed 1:1 line? Right around tuning. Left of that point the reported thickness is essentially constant — it has become a property of your wavelet, not of the rock. Isochron-based thickness maps are meaningless below this value.
  3. Buy resolution with frequency. Push peak frequency from 20 Hz to 60 Hz and watch the tuning thickness in the readout. It scales as 1/f. Tripling frequency thins the resolvable bed threefold — which is exactly why broadband processing is worth paying for, and why deep targets are so much harder.
  4. Add some honesty. Set signal-to-noise to about 4 and look for the thin end of the wedge. The λ/8 detection limit assumes noiseless data. With real noise you lose the thin end long before λ/8. Detectability is a data-quality question, not just a physics question.
  5. Flip the base contrast to "Same". A bed whose top and base have the same sign behaves in reverse: the two reflections cancel near λ/4 and reinforce as the bed vanishes. Same rock thickness, opposite amplitude behaviour. This is why you must know your polarity and your impedance profile before you interpret any amplitude anomaly.

What to carry away

  • Resolving and detecting are different jobs. Around λ/4 you stop being able to separate top from base. Down to roughly λ/8 you can still tell that something is there from the amplitude, if the data are clean.
  • Below tuning, amplitude is a thickness proxy — and a treacherous one. Amplitude falls roughly linearly with thickness there, so bright can mean thick. Above tuning that relationship inverts and then breaks.
  • A bright spot at tuning is not more rock. It is the same rock, interfering with itself. Channels routinely look brightest along their thin edges.
  • λ = V/f, so resolution is worst exactly where you usually want it most: deep, where velocity is high and the earth has already absorbed your high frequencies.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Each trace is a 1D convolutional model: two reflection coefficients, convolved with a zero-phase wavelet. The convolution is done analytically as a sum of shifted, scaled wavelets rather than on a sample grid, so the reflection timing is exact and the tuning curves come out smooth instead of stair-stepped.

Amplitude and apparent thickness are picked from the synthetic the same way you would pick them in an interpretation package: the extremum matching the sign of the top reflection, and the time between that extremum and the one matching the base, refined by parabolic interpolation. Apparent thickness converts to length with the same interval velocity, h = V·Δt/2.

Deliberately left out, because each deserves its own module: wavelet phase other than zero, offset and NMO stretch, anything 3D, transmission losses and multiples inside the bed, and lateral velocity change. This is the textbook case, which is the point — the real world is worse, not better.
