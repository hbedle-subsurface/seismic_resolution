Module 06  ·  Spatial sampling

Is that dipping event real, or aliased?

A dipping seismic event is spatially aliased when its time shift from one trace to the next exceeds half a cycle at the frequency being considered. The event does not necessarily disappear — instead, frequencies above the spatial Nyquist limit fold into the sampled spectrum and can appear at the wrong apparent dip.

Try this

  1. Break it slowly. Leave the dip at 20° and increase trace spacing from 5 m to 80 m. Watch the time shift from trace to trace and compare it with half a cycle at the frequency you are considering. Once the spatial Nyquist frequency falls below the peak frequency, the peak itself is spatially aliased.
  2. Watch the fold in panel 3. Follow the energy in the f-k panel as the trace spacing becomes coarser. A planar event produces energy along a line through the origin. Once that line reaches the spatial Nyquist boundary, higher spatial frequencies fold back into the sampled spectrum. The apparent slope can therefore change sign.
  3. Alias by raising frequency alone. Fix dip and spacing, then increase peak frequency. The spatial Nyquist frequency is determined by trace spacing and the event's time dip. Increasing seismic frequency does not change that Nyquist limit; instead, more of the wavelet spectrum moves above it.
  4. Find the trade. Set spacing to 40 m and find the steepest dip that can be sampled at 30 Hz. Then repeat at 60 Hz. Doubling frequency roughly halves the allowable trace spacing for the same dip, or halves the steepest dip that can be sampled at the same spacing.
  5. Make it a fast-velocity target. Keep dip and spacing fixed and increase velocity. The same geometric dip produces a smaller time dip in a faster medium. The event therefore changes less in time from one trace to the next and is less vulnerable to spatial aliasing.
  6. Then take the quiz. Run ten cases from the recorded panel alone. This is closer to the real interpretation problem: there is no truth panel on a field dataset. You need to know the acquisition geometry and understand where the spatial Nyquist limit lies.

What to carry away

  • The key comparison is simple: compare the time shift from one trace to the next with half a cycle at the frequency of interest.
  • The central equation is dx ≤ V / (4 · f · sin θ) for a planar reflector in this constant-velocity, zero-offset model.
  • Spatial Nyquist is fNx = V / (4 · dx · sin θ) so frequencies above that value are spatially aliased.
  • Aliasing does not necessarily make an event disappear. It makes different continuous spatial frequencies indistinguishable after sampling.
  • High-frequency aliasing can happen before peak-frequency aliasing. A survey may still sample the peak of the wavelet while already folding some of its higher-frequency energy.
  • Steep, shallow and slow is the dangerous combination. Fault planes, salt flanks, channel edges and other steep events can have very large time dips.
  • You cannot generally recover information that was never uniquely sampled. Interpolation and processing can help when assumptions are available, but acquisition spacing sets the fundamental spatial sampling limit.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The synthetic reflector is a single planar event convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet. For a constant-velocity, zero-offset section, its two-way time slope is p = dt/dx = 2 sin θ / V seconds per metre.

The time shift between adjacent traces is therefore Δtx = p · dx and spatial Nyquist requires Δtx ≤ 1/(2f) for the frequency being considered.

Combining those equations gives dx ≤ V/(4f sin θ) or, equivalently, fNx = V/(4 dx sin θ).

The quoted "useful bandwidth" is not treated as a hard physical cutoff. A Ricker wavelet has no finite maximum frequency; its spectrum simply decreases away from the peak. The module therefore estimates a useful bandwidth from the actual wavelet spectrum rather than declaring an arbitrary maximum frequency.

Panel 3 is a two-dimensional Fourier transform of the recorded panel. A cosine taper is applied in time and space before the transform. For a planar event, the f-k energy follows a straight line whose slope is controlled by the event's time dip p = dt/dx rather than by the geological dip angle itself.

The densely sampled reference is a visualization reference: it uses much smaller trace spacing than the survey panel so that the continuous event can be seen without the survey's spatial sampling limitation. It is not an additional measurement.

This simplified model assumes a planar reflector, constant velocity, zero-offset geometry, and a single event. Real seismic data also involve finite bandwidth, variable velocity, migration, irregular sampling, noise, multiples, diffractions, and multiple dipping events.

Migration and interpolation can modify the practical aliasing behavior, but they cannot create independent spatial information that was never uniquely sampled in the original acquisition.
