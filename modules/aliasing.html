Module 06  ·  Spatial sampling

Is that dipping event real, or aliased?

A dipping reflector changes its arrival time from trace to trace. If that change becomes larger than half a period at a frequency present in the data, the spatial sampling cannot uniquely represent the dip. The event does not disappear — it can fold into a different, apparently real dip.

Try this

  1. Break it slowly. Leave the dip at 20° and increase trace spacing. Watch the recorded panel. The trace-to-trace time step eventually becomes too large to represent the highest frequencies without folding.
  2. Change frequency. Hold dip and spacing fixed and increase frequency. Higher frequencies have shorter periods, so they are the first frequencies to violate the spatial sampling criterion.
  3. Change velocity. Keep dip and spacing fixed and increase velocity. The same geometric dip produces a smaller time shift per metre when velocity increases, making spatial aliasing less likely.
  4. Watch the spectrum. Look at the frequency-wavenumber panel. The dipping event follows a line in f-k space. Once that line reaches the spatial Nyquist boundary, it folds back.
  5. Take the quiz. Hide the answer and judge the recorded panel alone. On real seismic data there is no truth panel. Survey geometry and knowledge of the spatial bandwidth are therefore essential.

What to carry away

  • The key quantity is time shift per trace. For a planar reflector: Δt = 2 dx sinθ / V .
  • Spatial aliasing begins when: Δt > 1/(2f) .
  • Rearranging gives: dx < V/(4 f sinθ) .
  • Aliasing does not necessarily make an event disappear. It can make the event appear with the wrong dip.
  • Increasing frequency, increasing dip, or decreasing velocity makes spatial aliasing more likely.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The synthetic is a single planar reflector convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet. The two-way-time slope is p = 2 sinθ / V in seconds per metre. Therefore the time shift between adjacent traces is Δt = p dx .

Spatial aliasing occurs when the phase change between adjacent spatial samples exceeds π radians: 2π f p dx > π . Therefore: f > 1/(2p dx) . Equivalently: dx < V/(4 f sinθ) .

The truth panel is sampled eight times more finely than the recorded panel. It is a visualization of the underlying synthetic reflector, not a claim about what a real survey sampled.

The f-k panel is calculated from the actual recorded panel using a two-dimensional FFT. A cosine taper is applied in time and space before the transform to reduce edge leakage.

This simple demonstration does not include migration anti-alias operators, interpolation, irregular geometry, noise, multiples, diffraction, or the more complicated sampling requirements of three-dimensional surveys.
