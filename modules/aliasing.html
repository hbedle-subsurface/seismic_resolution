Module 06  ·  Spatial sampling

Is that dipping event real, or aliased?

A reflector that steps down by more than half a wavelet period from one trace to the next cannot be reconstructed. The data does not go blank — it shows you an event with the wrong dip, and often the wrong sign of dip, drawn just as confidently as a real one. Three things decide it: how far apart your traces are, how steeply the event dips, and how high your frequencies go.

Test yourself

You only get the recorded panel. Decide whether the dip you can see is the real one.

Try this

  1. Break it slowly. Leave the dip at 20° and walk trace spacing out from 5 m to 80 m, watching panel 2 and the step-per-trace number. Nothing much happens until the step passes half a period. After that the event starts to look ragged, then reverses. The number to watch is the step from trace to trace against half a period — that comparison, and nothing else, is what aliasing means.
  2. Watch the fold in panel 3. With the spacing coarse, follow the dashed line up from the origin. It runs straight until it reaches the edge of the plot, which is the wavenumber Nyquist, then reappears on the opposite side and comes back with the opposite sign. That is "folding back" literally: energy that belongs off the edge of the plot has nowhere to go, so it wraps around and lands on top of a dip that was never there.
  3. Alias by raising frequency alone. Fix dip and spacing, then push peak frequency up. Broader bandwidth does not only buy resolution, it also costs you sampling. The high end of the wavelet aliases first, which is why an aliased event often looks fine at low frequency and falls apart when the processor tries to boost the high end.
  4. Find the trade. Set spacing to 40 m and find the steepest dip you can record cleanly at 30 Hz, then at 60 Hz. Doubling the frequency roughly halves the steepest dip you can record. This is the calculation behind survey design: you choose your trace spacing from the steepest dip you expect and the highest frequency you hope to keep.
  5. Make it a fast-velocity target. Take velocity from 2000 to 5000 m/s at a fixed dip. Aliasing gets easier to avoid, because what matters is the time step per trace, not the geometric dip. The same geological dip is gentler in time when the rock is fast. Salt flanks are hard for the opposite reason.
  6. Then take the quiz. Run ten cases from the recorded panel alone. This is the honest version of the question, because on real data there is no truth panel. The only defences are knowing your acquisition geometry, and looking at the spectrum before you believe a steep event.

What to carry away

  • The rule is one comparison: the time step from one trace to the next against half a period of your highest frequency. If the step wins, the event is aliased.
  • Written out, that is dx < V / (4 · f · sin θ) — trace spacing, velocity, frequency and dip, all in one line.
  • Aliased data does not look empty, it looks wrong. It shows a confident event at a dip that does not exist, and it can point the opposite way.
  • Steep, shallow and slow is the dangerous combination. Fault planes, salt flanks, channel edges and steep limbs are where it bites.
  • You cannot fix it later. Once two dips are indistinguishable in the recording, no processing can separate them — which is why this is decided when the survey is designed.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

A single plane reflector is convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet. Its time dip is p = 2 sin θ / V seconds per meter, so the step from one trace to the next is p · dx. Aliasing begins when that step reaches half a period, p · dx > 1/(2f), which rearranges to the familiar dx < V/(4 f sin θ). The frequency quoted as "aliased above" is 1/(2 p dx).

Panel 3 is a real two-dimensional Fourier transform of panel 2, not a sketch: a cosine taper in both directions, then an FFT in time and another in space. A dipping event maps to a straight line through the origin whose slope is its dip, so the wrapping you see at the edges is the actual recorded energy, arriving where the transform puts it.

The truth panel is the same reflector sampled eight times more finely. No survey samples that well; it is there so you can see what the recording is failing to capture.

Left out: anti-alias filtering in migration operators, which limits dip rather than curing the recording; interpolation schemes that can partly recover aliased data when the dip is known; and the fact that noise and multiple dips make this far harder to spot than it is here. The steepest dip that matters is usually on the fault planes and diffractions, not the reflectors.
