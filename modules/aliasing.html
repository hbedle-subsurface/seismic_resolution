Module 06  ·  Spatial sampling

Is that dipping event real, or aliased?

A dipping reflector can only be sampled correctly if the time shift from one trace to the next is smaller than half a period of the highest frequency you want to preserve. When the trace spacing is too large, the event does not disappear — it folds into a different apparent dip.

Try this

  1. Break it slowly. Leave the dip at 20° and walk trace spacing out from 5 m to 80 m. Watch the step from trace to trace. Once it becomes larger than half a period at a given frequency, that frequency is spatially aliased.
  2. Watch the fold in panel 3. Increase the trace spacing. Energy moves toward the spatial Nyquist limit and then folds back into the recorded wavenumber range.
  3. Raise frequency. Fix dip and spacing, then increase frequency. Higher frequencies alias first because they have shorter wavelengths and therefore require finer spatial sampling.
  4. Find the trade. Set spacing to 40 m and compare 30 Hz with 60 Hz. Doubling frequency approximately halves the maximum recordable dip for a fixed trace spacing and velocity.
  5. Change velocity. Increase velocity from 2000 to 5000 m/s. The same geometric dip produces a smaller time shift between traces in a faster medium.
  6. Then take the quiz. On real seismic there is no truth panel. Acquisition geometry and spectral information are part of your evidence.

What to carry away

  • The rule: the time step from one trace to the next must be smaller than half a period of the frequency you want to preserve.
  • The sampling equation: dx ≤ V / (4 · f · sin θ)
  • Aliased data does not look empty. It can look like a perfectly coherent event with the wrong dip.
  • Steep + slow + high frequency is the dangerous combination.
  • You cannot recover information that was never sampled.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

A single plane reflector is represented by a zero-phase Ricker wavelet whose arrival time varies with horizontal position as

t(x) = t₀ + 2x sin θ / V

Therefore the two-way-time slope is

p = dt/dx = 2 sin θ / V

and the time shift from one trace to the next is

Δt = p · dx

Spatial aliasing begins when the phase change between adjacent traces reaches π radians:

Δt ≥ 1 / (2f)

Combining those expressions gives the familiar maximum trace spacing:

dx ≤ V / (4f sin θ)

The corresponding alias frequency for a particular dip and trace spacing is

falias = 1 / (2p dx) = V / (4 dx sin θ)

The truth panel is sampled much more finely than the recorded panel. It is a visualization of the continuous model, not a claim about a real acquisition system.

The f–k panel is calculated from the recorded traces using a two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform. Spatial wavenumber is expressed in cycles/m and the Nyquist wavenumber is

kNyquist = 1 / (2 dx)

This simplified demonstration does not include anti-alias filters, migration dip limits, interpolation, irregular acquisition, noise, multiples, or diffraction effects.
