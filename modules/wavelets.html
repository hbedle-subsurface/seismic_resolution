What your data is made of

Every trace on every module in this site is a wavelet, repeated once per reflector and added up. The wavelet's peak frequency gets all the attention because it is the one number on the acquisition report, but peak frequency alone does not fix how a wavelet behaves — its bandwidth does at least as much of the work. Build two wavelets that share a peak frequency and give them different bandwidths, and watch them disagree about the same bed.

Try this

  1. Start from the default pairing. Wavelet A is a 30 Hz Ricker, wavelet B an 8/16/42/60 Ormsby. Read the dominant frequency off each stat. They land within a hertz or two of each other. If you only read the peak frequency off a processing report, you would call these the same wavelet. They are not.
  2. Now compare the shaded band under each spectrum. The shaded region is the −6 dB bandwidth, the range over which the wavelet actually carries useful energy. The two bands differ even though the peaks line up, and it is that width, not the peak, that sets how sharp the wavelet is in time.
  3. Walk the bed thickness down from 60 m to zero and watch both wedges together. Both reflectors merge into one eventually, but not at the same thickness, and the brightening on the way down — the tuning effect — peaks at different beds for A and B. A wider bandwidth generally tunes at a thinner bed, because it packs more of a true impulse response into less time.
  4. Read the tuning-curve panel at the thickness where each curve peaks. That peak, not λ/4, is the actual thinnest bed each wavelet can double in amplitude on. λ/4 is a convenient rule of thumb derived assuming a simple band-limited wavelet; the measured tuning thickness moves around with real bandwidth, which is the whole point of comparing a Ricker against an Ormsby instead of trusting the formula blindly.
  5. Set the test-bed slider to a thickness between the two tuning thicknesses. One wavelet reads that bed as tuned and bright, the other reads it as still separating into two events. Same rock, same velocity, same day of acquisition — a different instrument gives a different verdict.
  6. Now make wavelet B a Ricker too, matched to A's peak frequency. Set B's shape to Ricker and its frequency to the same value as A. The two wedges become identical, the two tuning curves overlap exactly, and the two-trace panel collapses to one line. Bandwidth was the only thing doing any work in the earlier comparison — once shape and peak frequency both match, there is nothing left to disagree about.
  7. Push wavelet B's low cut up toward its low plateau, squeezing the ramp into almost a step. A steep-sided passband rings: watch side lobes appear beside the main event in the time-domain panel and in the traces below the wedge. A sharp box in frequency is a slowly decaying oscillation in time — narrowing the transition bands buys you a crisper-looking passband and a noisier-looking wavelet, and real processing has to trade the two off.
  8. Drop wavelet A's peak frequency to 15 Hz and raise B's high cut to 90 Hz, keeping B's low cut near 20 Hz. Now the dominant frequencies are close to a factor of two apart in the other direction and the bandwidths overlap only partially. This is closer to what actually happens comparing two real surveys, or a legacy 2D line against a modern 3D: the whole spectrum moves, not just the label on the report.

What to carry away

  • Peak frequency is one number; a wavelet is a shape. Two wavelets can share a peak frequency and disagree about everything downstream of it — tuning thickness, side-lobe strength, apparent bed boundary position.
  • Bandwidth, not peak frequency, sets resolving power. A broader passband concentrates the wavelet in time and pushes the tuning thickness down, which is why acquisition and processing reports are more honest when they quote a band, not a single Hertz value.
  • λ/4 is a rule of thumb, not a law. It comes from idealizing the wavelet. Every later module on this site uses it as a convenient benchmark, but the tuning curve here shows the real answer moving around it depending on what the wavelet actually looks like.
  • Ringing is the price of a sharp passband. Steepen an Ormsby's corners and you buy a cleaner frequency response and a wavelet with more side-lobe energy — resolution and ringing trade against each other, not for free.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Both wavelets are zero phase and normalized so their central sample equals 1, so a single isolated reflection reads at amplitude equal to the reflection coefficient regardless of which wavelet made it. The Ricker is the second derivative of a Gaussian, fixed by one number, its peak frequency. The Ormsby is a trapezoid in the amplitude spectrum defined by four corner frequencies — low cut, low plateau, high plateau, high cut — and is the shape most processing shops actually apply as a band-pass filter, because real acquisition and real anti-alias filters roll off gradually rather than obeying a single peak.

The wedge is the same two-reflector model used elsewhere on this site: a bed of variable thickness bounded by equal and opposite reflection coefficients, so it thins to a perfect cancellation at zero thickness rather than to a single isolated event. The composite-amplitude curve is read directly off that wedge, one column at a time, as the peak absolute amplitude of the column divided by the reflection coefficient. A value of 1 means the two reflectors are acting independently; a value near 2 means they are interfering constructively at close to their maximum; the curve's own peak, not a formula, is reported as each wavelet's measured tuning thickness.

The −6 dB bandwidth shown under each spectrum is the frequency range over which the amplitude spectrum stays at or above half its peak value. It is one of several reasonable ways to define "bandwidth" for a wavelet with no hard edges, chosen here because it is the convention most seismic processing reports already use.

This is a 1-D convolutional model with no attenuation, no multiples, and no NMO stretch. It leaves those effects to be layered on top; what it isolates is the one relationship this module is about, between a wavelet's shape in frequency and its behavior in time.
