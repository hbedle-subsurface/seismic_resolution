Module 08  ·  Phase and polarity

Am I picking the top of the sand, or near it?

Every other module has assumed a zero-phase wavelet, where the peak sits exactly on the boundary that made it. Real data is rarely quite zero phase. Rotate the wavelet here and watch the event slide away from the rock that produced it, while looking every bit as crisp and confident as before. Nothing warns you. The horizon is simply in the wrong place.

The assumption everything else has been making

Every module before this one used a zero-phase wavelet. That means its biggest peak sits exactly on the boundary that produced it, so picking the peak is the same thing as picking the top of the sand.

That is what processing aims for, and it is worth aiming for. It is also an assumption, and one you inherit rather than check.

Rotating the phase slides the pulse sideways in time while leaving its frequency content alone. The event stays just as sharp and just as loud. It simply stops being in the right place.

Why this is the one that costs money

A phase error becomes a depth error, in the same direction, on every horizon you pick, across the whole survey. It does not average out. At 30 Hz a 90° rotation puts your pick about 6 ms — eight or nine meters — off the rock.

And nothing on the section warns you. There is no fuzziness and no artifact. The horizon looks clean and it is wrong.

Which is what a well tie is actually for. It is not a formality — it is the measurement that tells you how much your data has been rotated. There is a matching game below; it is the closest thing here to the real job.

Try this — and what to watch for

  1. Start at zero phase and look at panel 2. Why bother: it is the case everybody draws on the whiteboard, and the one you should confirm rather than assume. Where is the peak relative to the teal line? Exactly on it. This is the case everyone draws on the whiteboard, and it is the reason zero phase is what processing aims for: the event and the rock are in the same place, so picking the peak means picking the top of the sand.
  2. Rotate to 30°, then 60°, then 90°, why bother: to see the error grow smoothly with nothing on the display flagging it. Watch the readout for how far the peak has moved. The event slides steadily away from the boundary — around 6 ms by 90°, which is eight or nine meters of depth at normal velocities. The event never looks wrong. It stays sharp and confident, just in the wrong place, and a horizon picked on it inherits the whole error.
  3. Stop at exactly 90° and look at the true boundary. Why bother: 90° data is common, and it is the worst possible case for tying a well. The amplitude there is zero. The boundary sits on a zero crossing with a peak on one side and a trough on the other, and the two are mirror images, so there is nothing in the data to tell you which one to pick. Ninety-degree data is the worst case for tying a well, and it is common, because that is what you get from minimum-phase data that has not been fully converted.
  4. Go to 180° and compare with the reverse polarity button. Why bother: these two get confused constantly, and here you can see they are the same operation. They are the same thing. A 180° rotation is a polarity flip, which is why the two get confused so often. Anything between 0 and 180 is a partial flip, and there is no name for it — only a number.
  5. Switch the well to the thin sand. Why bother: it stacks this module on top of module 02, which is what real reservoirs do to you. Now two problems compound. Tuning has already merged the top and base into one event, and phase then moves that merged event off both boundaries. Working out where the sand really is from the seismic alone becomes impossible, which is precisely why thin reservoirs need well control.
  6. Then tie a well properly. Why bother: this is the actual job, and the reveal tells you what your error would have cost in meters. Turn on the matching game and rotate until the two traces sit on top of each other. This is what a well tie is: finding the rotation that makes the synthetic and the seismic agree. Get it right and every horizon you pick is in the right place. Get it wrong by 40° and every depth you post is wrong by the same few meters, consistently, across the whole survey.

If you remember nothing else

  • Zero phase means the peak is on the boundary. That is the whole point of it, and it is an assumption you should check rather than inherit.
  • Phase error becomes depth error, systematically and in the same direction everywhere. It does not average out across a survey.
  • At 90° the boundary is a zero crossing, with a peak and a trough on either side and no way to choose between them from the amplitude.
  • 180° of phase and reversed polarity are the same operation. Knowing which convention your data uses is the first question, not a detail.
  • A well tie is a phase measurement. That is what it is for, and it is why an untied survey cannot give you reliable depths no matter how good the imaging.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Phase rotation is done exactly, in the frequency domain: the wavelet is transformed, positive frequencies are multiplied by e−iφ and negative ones by the conjugate, and it is transformed back. That is equivalent to w·cos φ − H{w}·sin φ, where H is the Hilbert transform. It is checked against the cases with known answers — a 0° rotation reproduces the wavelet, 180° negates it, and 90° comes out antisymmetric to within rounding.

The well is a stack of layers with impedances, exactly as in module 01, and the synthetic is the reflection coefficients convolved with the rotated wavelet. The pick shift quoted is the time of the largest positive lobe of the rotated wavelet, which is where an interpreter tracking a peak would put the horizon. Depth error uses the velocity you set, as V·Δt/2.

Left out: phase that varies with frequency, which is what most real data actually has and which no single rotation can fix; phase drift with depth as the earth absorbs high frequencies; the stretch and squeeze part of a real tie; and the fact that the sonic log itself needs correcting before any of this works. The single constant rotation used here is the easy case.

That is the whole set

Nine modules, one idea

Seismic does not show you rock. It shows you rock convolved with a wavelet, and that wavelet decides which beds you resolve, which faults you map, how big a feature has to be to survive, whether a bright spot means fluid or thickness, and whether your horizon sits on the boundary at all. Every limit you have met is some fraction of one wavelength — and the wavelength is V / f, which you can work out for your own target in about ten seconds.

If you take one habit away, let it be this: before trusting anything you have mapped, work out λ/4 at that depth, in that rock, with that data, and ask whether what you are looking at is above or below it.
