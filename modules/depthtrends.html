How the earth changes as you go down

If you are new to seismic, start here. Before any question about what you can and cannot see makes sense, you need a feel for the earth the sound is travelling through. Build a column below, from the sea surface down to five kilometres, and three things happen at once — all of which shape everything you will interpret later.

Three things happen as you go down

Rocks compact. Pile sediment on top of sediment and the grains pack tighter, water is squeezed out, and the rock gets both faster and denser. Velocity climbs roughly in a straight line with depth, and density follows it. That is the background trend, and most of the earth obeys it.

Time is not depth. Seismic arrives in two-way time. Because velocity keeps rising, a second of time near the surface covers far less rock than a second of time at four kilometres. Every thickness you read off a section in milliseconds has to be converted with a velocity that is itself changing.

The earth absorbs energy. It takes the high frequencies first, so the pulse that started sharp arrives at depth long and smooth. This is attenuation, measured by a number called Q, and it is the reason deep data always looks softer than shallow data.

Why you should care

Those three trends gang up on you. How much detail seismic can show depends on its wavelength, and wavelength is just velocity divided by frequency: λ = V / f. Going down, the velocity on top of that fraction gets bigger and the frequency underneath it gets smaller. Both push the wavelength up, and a longer wavelength means less detail.

So the thinnest bed you can resolve grows steadily with depth — often from ten metres near the surface to fifty or more at four kilometres. That is not a failure of processing or of the interpreter. It is the earth.

Drag the depth marker down and watch the last panel. It is the most useful plot on this page: it tells you the thinnest bed you could hope to see, at any depth, for the data you have. Keep that number in mind and every other module will make more sense.

Try this — and what to watch for

  1. Drag the depth marker from top to bottom and watch the last stat cell — the thinnest bed you could resolve. Why bother: it is the one number that explains why a prospect that looked crisp shallow turns to mush deep. The thinnest resolvable bed grows several times over between the shallow section and the deep one. Nothing about the equipment changed. This single number explains why a prospect that looked crisp at 1.5 s is a smear at 4 s.
  2. Find the overpressured zone in panel 2. It is the kick to the left in the velocity curve, where the rock stops getting faster. Why bother: spotting this early is one of the genuinely high-value things an interpreter does, and it is a drilling-safety issue. Compaction reverses because the pore fluid is carrying some of the weight the grains should be carrying, so the rock stays looser than its depth suggests. Velocity drops, density drops with it, and drilling into it without knowing is how wells get into trouble. On seismic it appears as a velocity inversion, and picking it early is one of the genuinely high-value things an interpreter does.
  3. Watch what it does to impedance in panel 4. There is a strong negative reflection at the top of the zone and a positive one at its base, because impedance falls and then recovers. That pair is often the clearest thing on the section, and it is a fluid-pressure boundary rather than a rock-type boundary.
  4. Turn the water depth down to zero. The whole time axis shifts up by the two-way time through the water, and the shear velocity curve now starts at the surface instead of at the seabed. Water carries no shear wave at all, which is why marine streamer data records only P waves.
  5. Take Q from 300 down to 50 and watch panels 6, 7 and 8 together. Why bother: it shows that a bad shallow section damages everything beneath it, which no processing fully undoes. The pulse visibly fattens and the resolvable bed thickness grows. Gassy or very soft shallow sediment can do this, and everything beneath it is degraded — the damage is done on the way through and no processing fully undoes it.
  6. Push the velocity gradient from 0.3 up to 1.0. A fast-compacting section reaches high velocity sooner, so it runs out of resolution sooner. Slow, undercompacted sections keep their resolution deeper. Two basins at the same depth can be very different targets.

If you remember nothing else

  • Velocity and density climb together with burial, and impedance climbs faster than either, because it is their product.
  • A millisecond is not a fixed thickness. It is worth more rock the deeper you go, because velocity keeps rising.
  • Overpressure reverses the trend, and shows up as a velocity and impedance inversion — one of the few places seismic tells you about fluids rather than rock.
  • Q describes how much energy the rock absorbs. High frequencies go first, so bandwidth narrows steadily on the way down.
  • Deep resolution is worse for two reasons at once, higher velocity and lower frequency, and both act on the same wavelength.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Velocity below the seabed follows a linear compaction trend, V = V₀ + k·z. Density comes from Gardner's relation, ρ = 0.31·V0.25 with V in m/s, which is the metric form of the original ft/s expression. Shear velocity uses Castagna's mudrock line, Vs = (Vp − 1360)/1.16. Water is 1500 m/s, 1.03 g/cc, and carries no shear wave. All three are rules of thumb that hold for clastic sections and fail for carbonates, evaporites and anything unusual — they are here to give the right shape, not the right number for your field.

The overpressured zone multiplies the velocity trend by a smooth raised-cosine trough. Density falls with it through Gardner, which gives a drop of a few per cent for a substantial velocity reversal, and that is about what is observed.

Two-way time is integrated down the column as Σ 2·dz/V. Attenuation is constant-Q: the amplitude spectrum is multiplied by exp(−π f t*) where t* = Σ dt/Q along the path, with water treated as lossless. For a Ricker source the surviving peak frequency then has a closed form, which is checked here against a numerical search of the attenuated spectrum and agrees to better than a tenth of a hertz.

What this does not include, and it matters: processing. Inverse-Q filtering and spectral whitening recover part of the lost bandwidth, so real processed data at depth usually looks better than the raw physics here suggests. What is shown is what the earth does before anybody tries to correct it. Also left out are velocity dispersion, which accompanies attenuation, and the anisotropy that makes real velocities depend on direction.
