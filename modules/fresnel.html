Module 04  ·  Horizontal resolution

How small a feature can I map in plan view?

Modules 01 to 03 asked how thin a bed you can see. This one asks how small a feature you can see, looking down on a map. The answer is set by the same wavelength, and by one processing step that most interpreters inherit without ever watching it work.

One trace does not see one point

Picture the sound pulse leaving the surface and spreading out as it goes down, like the beam of a torch widening. When it strikes the reflector it does not touch a single spot. It lights up a circular patch, and the echo you record is everything in that patch added together.

The patch is as wide as it is because echoes from slightly off to the side travel only slightly further, arrive at nearly the same moment, and pile onto the same part of the trace. The patch has a name — the Fresnel zone — and at ordinary depths it is hundreds of meters across, usually bigger than whatever you are trying to map.

So a single unmigrated trace is a very blunt instrument. It reports one number for an area the size of several football fields.

What migration does about it

Think of a camera pointed at a distant streetlight, with the lens out of focus. The single lamp spreads into a big soft disc. Nothing is wrong with the light and nothing is missing from the picture — the energy has simply been spread out, and it can be gathered back.

Turning the focus ring collapses that disc back to a small bright dot. Migration is the focus ring — it is the name of the processing step that does exactly this, taking the energy smeared across the Fresnel zone and putting it back where it came from. When someone says data is "migrated", this is what they mean has been done to it.

Panels 3 and 4 below show it happening on the section. Unmigrated, each feature draws a curved frown across many traces, and the frowns from neighbouring features overlap. Migration collapses each frown back to a point.

And exactly like a camera, there is a limit. However perfectly you focus, a lens cannot make a point smaller than its own diffraction limit, and migration cannot shrink a feature below about a quarter of a wavelength. That number should look familiar — it is the same λ/4 that limited bed thickness in module 02.

Where and what

Migration — the focus step

Migration is the processing step that does the focusing. 3D focuses in every direction. 2D focuses along the line only, because a single line samples only one direction.

Nearly everything handed to you today is migrated. Older vintages and some legacy 2D lines are not, so if you ever work a survey from the tape library, set this to Unmigrated and see what you would be interpreting.

Display

Migration gathers energy from traces either side. The less of the survey it has to work with, the softer the focus.

Where the blur comes from, on the section

A single small feature does not put its echo on one trace. It puts a little energy on every trace that can see it, which draws the curved frown you see on the left. Notice that the two frowns cross each other: energy from both features arrives on the same traces, mixed together. That overlap is the Fresnel zone seen side-on, and it is why the unmigrated map is a smear. Migration is the processing step that gathers each frown back to the point that made it.

Try this — and what to watch for

  1. Turn the focus ring. Why bother: it is the single biggest improvement anything in processing makes to a map. The page opens unmigrated, the way the data comes off the boat. Click 3D and watch panels 1 and 4. The blur drops from a few hundred meters across to a few tens. Everything you believe about the shape of a feature in map view — a channel edge, the limit of a sand, the trace of a fault — depends on that one step having worked.
  2. Slide them together. Why bother: it is the same λ/4 you met vertically, now acting sideways. Press the button and watch panel 2 and the slice below it. They stay two until the gap closes to about a quarter of a wavelength, then merge into one blob that gets brighter rather than wider. Two gas pockets become one. Two channels become one channel. Nothing in the map tells you which it was.
  3. Ask what the unfocused panel could ever tell you. Why bother: it shows what migration is actually for. Back on Unmigrated, separate the two features by 300 m and try to convince yourself there are two. You cannot. They sit inside the same Fresnel patch and no amount of staring, or gain, or colour will separate them. Migration is not a tidying-up step at the end. It is the step that makes lateral interpretation possible at all.
  4. Go deeper. Why bother: feature sizes are not comparable between a shallow field and a deep one. Take the horizon from 1.2 s down to 3 s. The unfocused patch grows and the focused limit worsens too, though more slowly. The deeper the target, the more you are relying on migration having done its job.
  5. Shrink the survey. Why bother: it is why surveys are shot larger than the prospect, and why the edges of one are less trustworthy. Pull the survey width down to 600 m. The focus goes soft. A camera lens gathers light across its whole opening, and a narrow opening cannot focus sharply. Migration works the same way, which is why surveys are shot larger than the prospect, and why features near the edge of a survey are less trustworthy than the ones in the middle.
  6. Switch to 2D. Why bother: on a 2D grid, the shape of what you map is set partly by the direction you shot it. Look at the shape of the blur in panel 1. It becomes a cigar rather than a circle — sharp along the line, still smeared across it. Whether you can separate two features now depends on which way they happen to lie relative to your line. That is a geological conclusion being decided by the survey direction.

If you remember nothing else

  • Unmigrated, a trace reports one number for a patch hundreds of meters across. The Fresnel zone is (V/2)·√(t/f) in radius.
  • Migration is the focusing step, and it does most of the work — often a tenfold improvement. Almost all modern data is migrated; older vintages may not be, and it is worth checking rather than assuming.
  • λ/4 is the floor, not a promise. A narrow survey or a poor velocity model leaves you well short of it.
  • Two features closer than that image as one, and the merged blob is brighter, which makes it look more real rather than less.
  • Vertical and horizontal resolution end up in the same place. Both are a quarter of a wavelength, and both improve only with bandwidth.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The section panels are computed rather than sketched: each small feature produces a diffraction arc at τ(x) = √(t₀² + (2Δx/V)²), convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet and weighted by obliquity. That is exactly what a zero-offset section over a small feature looks like, and it is why migration has arcs to collapse.

The focused panels are not a full migration. They are the band-limited response that a successful migration produces: each feature images as a spot whose two-feature resolving distance is λ/(4 sin θ), with θ = arctan(survey width / depth). This is the standard resolution result. It is used here in preference to running a real migration operator because a real one brings its own artifacts, which would distract from the point. The trade-off is that these panels show what migration is trying to achieve rather than the ways it can fail.

The spot shape is calibrated numerically so that two features exactly the quoted distance apart are on the point of separating — otherwise the picture and the number in the stat strip would disagree, which they did by a factor of nearly three before the calibration was added.

The Fresnel zone quoted is the first Fresnel zone at zero offset, equivalently r ≈ √(λz/2). Left out: velocity error, dip, 3D acquisition footprint, and the fact that migration only focuses correctly when the velocity model is right. A football field is taken as 100 yards, about 91 m.
