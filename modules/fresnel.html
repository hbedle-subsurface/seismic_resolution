Module 04  ·  Horizontal resolution

How small a feature can I map in plan view?

Vertical resolution gets all the attention, but a seismic trace does not sample a point on the reflector. It samples a patch — the Fresnel zone — which at depth can be hundreds of meters across. Migration exists largely to shrink that patch, and it does so dramatically. What it cannot do is shrink it below about a quarter of a wavelength, or shrink it at all in a direction you did not acquire.

Try this

  1. See what migration is for. With the default setup, switch between None and 3D and watch panel 1. The patch each point smears into drops from a few hundred meters across to a few tens. Nearly everything you believe about the lateral shape of a feature — a channel edge, a fault trace, the limit of a sand body — depends on that collapse having worked.
  2. Walk the two points together. Press the button and watch panel 3 and the profile below. They stay separate until their spacing drops to about a quarter of a wavelength, then merge into a single blob that keeps getting brighter. This is the wedge model's tuning behaviour, rotated into the horizontal plane — same physics, same λ/4.
  3. Try to resolve them without migrating. Set migration to None and separate the points by 100 m. Unmigrated, they are inside one Fresnel zone and there is no separating them at any amplitude. Migration does not merely tidy the picture; it is the step that makes lateral interpretation possible at all.
  4. Go deep. Take the reflector time from 0.8 s to 3 s. The Fresnel zone grows as the square root of depth, but migrated resolution only worsens through λ, which grows more slowly. So migration buys you the most where the target is deepest — and deep unmigrated data is nearly useless laterally.
  5. Starve the aperture. Pull the migration aperture down to 400 m at 2 s. Resolution collapses, because the aperture sets the largest angle available and the migrated spot size goes as λ/(4 sin θ). A narrow survey, or a target near the edge of one, cannot be imaged sharply no matter how good the processing. This is why surveys are shot larger than the prospect.
  6. Switch from 3D to 2D. Look at the plan view. The spot becomes a cigar. A 2D line collapses the Fresnel zone along the line and leaves it untouched across the line, so a feature is resolved in one direction and smeared in the other. Anything you map from 2D lines carries this shape, and it is oriented by the acquisition rather than by the geology.

What to carry away

  • An unmigrated trace samples a patch, not a point. The Fresnel zone is (V/2)·√(t/f) in radius, which is hundreds of meters at typical depths.
  • Migration is what makes maps possible. It collapses that patch toward λ/4 — often a tenfold improvement or more.
  • λ/4 is the floor, not the guarantee. Limited aperture, wrong velocities and dipping overburden all leave you short of it.
  • Horizontal and vertical resolution are the same problem. Both end up at a quarter of a wavelength, and both improve only with bandwidth.
  • 2D lines resolve in one direction only. The smear is oriented by the survey, which is a good reason to be suspicious of feature shapes mapped from a grid of 2D lines.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The unmigrated panel is computed rather than sketched: each point scatterer produces a diffraction hyperbola at τ(x) = √(t₀² + (2Δx/V)²), convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet and weighted by obliquity. That is exactly what a zero-offset section over a point scatterer looks like.

The migrated panel is not a full Kirchhoff migration. It is the band-limited point-spread response that a successful migration produces: each scatterer images as its wavelet in time, multiplied by a lateral response whose half-amplitude radius is λ / (4 sin θ), where θ is the largest angle the aperture allows, θ = arctan(aperture / depth). This is the standard resolution result, and it is used here in preference to running a real migration operator because a real one brings operator aliasing and edge artifacts that would obscure the point being made. The trade-off is that this panel shows what migration is trying to achieve rather than the ways it can fail.

The Fresnel zone quoted is the first Fresnel zone at zero offset, r = (V/2)·√(t/f), equivalently r ≈ √(λz/2). Real apertures are limited by the survey, by dip, and by how far the processor is willing to let noise in. Also left out: 3D acquisition footprint, azimuthal variation, velocity error, and the fact that migration only focuses correctly when the velocity model is right.
