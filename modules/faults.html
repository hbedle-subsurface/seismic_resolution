Module 03  ·  Vertical resolution in practice

Can you see the fault?

For fault throw calculations, you need to know the vertical resolution. Below about a quarter of a wavelength the offset stops looking like a step — but that doesn't mean there isn't a fault there. It is hiding in the waveform. If you want to test your fault detecting skills --> turn on quiz mode and find out how good your eye actually is!

Try this

  1. Walk the throw down. Start at 40 m and reduce it. At what throw does the offset stop reading as a clean step in the reflectors? Somewhere near λ/4. Above it you see displacement; below it you see a smear.
  2. Now keep going, and watch the lower panel. Take the throw down to 5 m and compare the two traces. The step is long gone from the section, but the two traces are still visibly different — the waveform is distorted where the reflections interfere across the fault. That residual is what coherence, curvature and similarity attributes are built to find. Your eye gives up before the maths does.
  3. Trade frequency against throw. Fix the throw at 12 m and sweep frequency from 15 Hz to 70 Hz. The same fault goes from invisible to obvious without a single rock changing. "Is there a fault here?" is not only a geology question — it is a question about your bandwidth.
  4. Make it deep. Raise velocity to 5000 m/s and drop frequency to 15 Hz, as if you had gone from 1 s to 4 s two-way time. λ balloons past 300 m. A 40 m throw that was unmissable at shallow depth becomes marginal. Deep fault maps are systematically less complete than shallow ones, and it is not the interpreter's fault.
  5. Coarsen the acquisition. Push trace spacing to 50 m at a steep dip. The fault plane becomes a staircase. Spatial sampling limits what you can see laterally just as bandwidth limits what you can see vertically.
  6. Then be honest with yourself. Turn on quiz mode and run ten cases. Roughly a third of cases have no fault at all. Noise makes convincing fault-like wiggles, and knowing a fault "should" be there is how it gets mapped whether or not it exists.

What to carry away

  • Throw is a thickness problem in disguise. The same λ/4 and λ/8 numbers from the wedge model govern whether a fault reads as an offset.
  • Invisible is not absent. Sub-resolution faults still perturb the waveform. That is the entire premise of seismic attributes — and why an attribute can be right when the amplitude display looks blank.
  • Noise sets the real limit. The textbook limits assume clean data. At 3:1 you will miss faults you would have picked easily at 15:1, and you will invent a few that aren't there.
  • Fault maps are bandwidth maps. Two vintages of data over the same field will disagree about how many faults exist. Usually neither is lying.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

A stack of horizontal reflectors is offset across a planar normal fault. Each trace is built by convolving its reflectivity with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet, then band-limited noise is added — noise made by convolving random reflectivity with the same wavelet, so it looks like seismic rather than static. Signal-to-noise is quoted as the ratio of reflection-coefficient amplitude to noise RMS.

This is a post-migration cartoon. It deliberately omits fault-plane reflections, tip diffractions, drag folding, juxtaposition-driven amplitude change, and the smearing that imperfect migration puts on every real fault. All of those make faults easier to spot in some situations and harder in others. The point here is the resolution floor, which none of them removes.
