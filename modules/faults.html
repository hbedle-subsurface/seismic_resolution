Module 03  ·  Vertical resolution in practice

Can you see the fault?

Fault throw runs into the same resolution limit as bed thickness. Once the throw falls below about a quarter of a wavelength the reflectors stop showing a clean offset, but the fault has not gone anywhere: it shows up as a change in waveform instead of a step. Quiz mode hides the answer so you can test your eye against it.

Try this

  1. Walk the throw down. Start at 40 m and reduce it. Where does the offset stop reading as a clean step in the reflectors? Somewhere close to λ/4. Above that you can measure the throw off the section. Below it you can only say that something happened.
  2. Keep going, and watch the trace panel. Take the throw down to 5 m and compare the two traces. The step has gone from the section, but the traces still differ. Reflections interfering across the fault distort the waveform. Coherence and similarity attributes are built to find that residual, which is why an attribute can flag a fault that looks like nothing on the amplitude display.
  3. Trade frequency against throw. Fix the throw at 12 m and sweep frequency from 15 Hz to 70 Hz. The geology never changes, and the fault goes from invisible to obvious. Whether you can see a fault depends on your bandwidth as much as on the rocks.
  4. Make it deep. Raise velocity to 5000 m/s and drop frequency to 15 Hz, roughly the change from 1 s to 4 s two-way time. λ goes past 300 m, and a 40 m throw that was obvious at shallow depth becomes marginal. Deep fault maps are less complete than shallow ones for reasons that have nothing to do with the interpreter.
  5. Coarsen the acquisition. Push trace spacing to 50 m with a shallow fault dip. The fault plane turns into a staircase. Trace spacing limits lateral detail the same way bandwidth limits vertical detail.
  6. Thin the beds. Set the thinnest bed to 8 m and the thickest to 20 m, with frequency at 25 Hz. The beds are now below tuning, so the reflectors merge and interfere. Tracking a fault through tuned reflections is much harder than tracking one through clean, well-separated ones, and most reservoir intervals look like the former.
  7. Run ten quiz cases. About a third of them contain no fault. Correlated noise produces convincing fault-like breaks, and going in expecting a fault is a reliable way to map one that isn't there.

What to carry away

  • Throw and bed thickness hit the same limit. The λ/4 and λ/8 numbers from the wedge model apply here without modification.
  • A fault you cannot see is not necessarily absent. Sub-resolution throw still distorts the waveform, and that distortion is what seismic attributes are designed to pick up.
  • Noise sets the practical limit. Textbook resolution limits assume clean data. At 3:1 you will miss faults you would pick easily at 15:1, and you will call a few that aren't there.
  • Two vintages over the same field will map different numbers of faults. That is usually a difference in bandwidth rather than a disagreement about the geology.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

A stack of horizontal reflectors is offset across a planar normal fault. Bed thicknesses are drawn at random from the range you set and converted to two-way time using the interval velocity, so moving the velocity slider stretches the same geology rather than generating new geology. Each trace is the reflectivity convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet. The noise is band-limited (random reflectivity through the same wavelet) and correlated from trace to trace, so it resembles noise on a migrated section rather than static. Signal-to-noise is quoted as reflection-coefficient amplitude over noise RMS.

This is a post-migration cartoon. It leaves out fault-plane reflections, tip diffractions, drag folding, amplitude changes from juxtaposition, and the smearing that imperfect migration puts on every real fault. Those effects make faults easier to see in some settings and harder in others, but none of them removes the resolution floor, which is what this module is about.

The display is vertically exaggerated, as nearly all seismic displays are. The readout gives the fault dip as drawn alongside its true dip so you can compare the two. Fault dips read off a section are routinely too steep for this reason.
