Does bright mean thick?

Module 02 showed one wedge. A channel is thousands of them side by side, and the amplitude map you extract from it inherits every bit of that behaviour. Here is a channel whose thickness you know exactly, turned into the amplitude extraction you would actually be handed. Compare the two maps and see which parts of the story survive.

Try this

  1. Look at the two maps before touching anything. Where is the channel brightest, and where is it actually thickest? They are not the same place. The upstream half is thicker than tuning, so its axis is dim and its flanks are bright — the channel images as two rails with a dull stripe down the middle. The thin downstream half behaves itself and is brightest along the axis. One channel, two opposite-looking amplitude signatures, and no change in rock type anywhere.
  2. Find the same amplitude twice. Put the crosshair on a bright flank, note the amplitude, then find somewhere else with the same value. The readout tells you which two thicknesses fit that amplitude. Below tuning, amplitude rises with thickness; above tuning it falls. One number, two answers, and nothing in the amplitude alone to tell you which. This is why amplitude cannot be inverted to thickness without an independent constraint.
  3. Watch the map lie. Switch the right-hand map to Inferred, then to Error. Inferred assumes the textbook rule that amplitude is proportional to thickness, which is only true below tuning. Where the channel is thicker than that, the map does not merely get vaguer — it puts the thin value on the thickest rock, and the error map shows the axis of the channel as the worst place in the survey.
  4. Make the problem disappear. Take the thickest point down until the whole channel is below tuning. Now amplitude really does track thickness and the two maps look alike. This is the case textbooks quote, and it is also the case in which the answer is least interesting, because the reservoir is thin everywhere.
  5. Buy your way out with bandwidth. Put the thickness back and raise frequency instead. Tuning thickness falls as 1/f, so more of the channel moves above tuning and the bright rails move outward toward the edges. Higher frequency does not remove tuning, it moves it — and it can turn a channel that looked simple into one that looks like two.
  6. Change the rocks, not the shape. Switch to hardening downward. With both reflection coefficients the same sign the tuning curve inverts, so the thinnest edges of the channel are now the brightest part of the map. The identical geometry produces an amplitude map with the opposite pattern. Before reading a bright spot as thick rock, you have to know your impedance profile and your polarity.

What to carry away

  • Bright means near tuning, not thick. Above tuning the relationship runs backwards.
  • Amplitude-to-thickness is double-valued. Every amplitude fits two thicknesses, and only well control, geological sense or an independent attribute tells you which.
  • Tuning makes channels look like they have edges they do not have. Bright rails along the flanks are an artifact of thickness passing through tuning, not a levee or a facies change.
  • Thickness maps made from amplitude are only safe below tuning, and you have to establish that before you trust them, not after.
  • Everything here is one wedge model repeated across a map. If module 02 made sense, this is the same result with consequences.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The channel is a sinuous body with an elliptical cross-section that thins downstream, so its thickness sweeps through the whole range from zero to the thickest point you set. At every map cell the thickness is converted to a two-way time with the channel velocity, and the response is the top and base reflection coefficients convolved with a zero-phase Ricker wavelet. The amplitude extraction is the extremum matching the sign of the top reflection, which is what a horizon-based extraction picks up. That is the wedge model of module 02, evaluated at every cell rather than along one line.

The inferred-thickness map applies the rule that gets taught first: amplitude is proportional to thickness, calibrated so that the tuning amplitude corresponds to the tuning thickness. That rule is sound below tuning and wrong above it, and the error map is the difference between it and the truth.

Left out, and each would make matters worse rather than better: lateral velocity change, a channel fill whose properties vary along it, dip and structural overprint, the fact that the extraction horizon is itself picked from tuned data, and interference from reflectors above and below. Noise here is added to the amplitude map rather than to the traces, which is a simplification.
