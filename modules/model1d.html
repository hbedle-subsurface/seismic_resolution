Where a seismic trace comes from

One column of rock. Give each layer a thickness, a velocity and a density, and the trace follows from there with nothing hidden: velocity times density is acoustic impedance, the contrast between two impedances is a reflection coefficient, and those coefficients convolved with a wavelet are the trace. Change any number on the left and watch which panel moves. Everything else on this site is this model, repeated sideways.

Try this

  1. Make a reflection disappear. Pick any interface and change the velocity and density of the layer below until its impedance matches the layer above. The reflection coefficient goes to zero and the event vanishes, even though the rock type still changes. Seismic sees impedance contrast, not geology. A sand and a shale with the same impedance are invisible to each other, and that is why some perfectly real formation tops have no reflector to pick.
  2. Separate velocity from density. Raise the velocity of one layer by 20%, then put it back and raise its density by 20% instead. Both change impedance and both change the reflection. Seismic cannot tell you which one did it — that ambiguity is the reason we need well logs, and the reason inversion is harder than it looks.
  3. Flip a reflection over. Make one layer slower and lighter than the one above it. The reflection coefficient goes negative and the event changes color. A soft layer over a hard one and a hard layer over a soft one produce opposite events. Getting this backwards is the classic well-tie error.
  4. Watch two reflections merge. Take the thinnest bed down from 40 m to 5 m, and keep an eye on panels 3, 4 and 5 together. The two spikes in panel 3 stay separate and keep their full size, because the rock is still there. In panel 4 their wavelets slide toward each other until they overlap almost completely, and panel 5 adds them into a single event. Nothing was lost in the rock or in the reflectivity — it was lost in the addition. That gap between what exists and what you can see is the whole subject of module 02.
  5. Follow one interface through the chain. Change the density of a single layer and track lane by lane which wavelet in panel 4 changes size. Only the lanes for the interfaces at the top and base of that layer respond, because a reflection coefficient belongs to a boundary and not to a layer. Each lane in panel 4 is the same wavelet every time, scaled by its coefficient and slid to its own time. That is all convolution is, and panel 5 is just those lanes added across.
  6. Make two wavelets cancel. Find a bed where the top and base coefficients have opposite signs, then thin it until the two lanes in panel 4 sit almost on top of each other. One is upside down relative to the other, so where they overlap they subtract. Thin enough and they nearly erase each other, which is why a very thin bed with strong contrasts can be almost invisible. Beds whose coefficients share a sign do the opposite and reinforce.
  7. Change the wavelet, not the rock. Leave the model alone and sweep frequency from 15 Hz to 70 Hz. Panels 1 to 3 never move, because the earth has not changed. Only the wavelets in panel 4 get narrower or broader, and the trace follows them. Every argument about what is or is not resolvable is an argument about the shape in panel 4.
  8. Switch polarity. The whole trace inverts and the color bar reverses with it. Nothing about the rock changed. Polarity is a convention about how data is displayed, and if you do not know which one your data uses, you cannot say whether a bright event is a hard streak or a soft one.

What to carry away

  • Impedance is the only rock property seismic responds to, and only through the contrast at a boundary. A thick uniform layer returns nothing from its middle.
  • Reflection coefficients are properties of interfaces, not of layers. They live at the boundaries and nowhere else.
  • The wavelet is where resolution is decided. The reflectivity in panel 3 is as sharp as the geology; the trace is only as sharp as the wavelet.
  • A trace is a sum, not a picture. Every sample in panel 5 is the total of every wavelet in panel 4 that reaches it. No single event on a seismic section belongs to one interface alone.
  • Convolution is a smearing operation, and it is not reversible in practice. Everything you interpret has been through it once already.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

Acoustic impedance is velocity times density. At an interface the normal-incidence reflection coefficient is R = (I₂ − I₁) / (I₂ + I₁), and the two-way time to each interface accumulates as 2h/V through the layers above it. The trace is the reflectivity convolved with a zero-phase wavelet, done here as a sum of shifted, scaled wavelets so interface timing is exact rather than rounded to the sample grid. The first interface is placed at 50 ms so the top reflection is clear of the start of the display.

This is normal incidence only, so there is no offset, no AVO, and no mode conversion; all three need the full Zoeppritz equations rather than the single expression above. Also left out: transmission loss through each interface, internal multiples, attenuation, and geometrical spreading. Each would reduce deeper amplitudes, and real processing tries to correct for them. The wavelet is zero phase, which is what processing aims for and rarely achieves exactly.
