Module 00  ·  Recap and bridge

Beyond normal incidence

This set assumes the seismic resolution modules, where impedance, the reflection coefficient, the wavelet and tuning are built from scratch. This module restates those four results in one place, names the assumption every one of them shared, and shows the measurement that assumption was hiding. It introduces nothing new about rocks. It is here so that module 01 can start from a specific question rather than from the beginning.

1Four results, restated

Everything this set takes as already established

The resolution modules built a chain from a column of rock to a seismic wiggle. Four of its results are used constantly from here on, and they are restated below so that the settings used later have a starting point.

Impedance is velocity times density. One number per layer, and at normal incidence it is the only rock property a reflection responds to.

A reflection measures a contrast. The reflection coefficient is the difference in impedance across the boundary divided by the sum. Equal impedances give no reflection, whatever else is different about the two rocks.

The sign is the polarity. Going into a higher impedance gives one sign, into a lower impedance the other, and which one appears as a peak on a display is a convention rather than a fact about the rock.

The trace is a sum. Each boundary returns a copy of the wavelet, and what is interpreted is the total. When two boundaries are close together their wavelets overlap and the amplitude that gets measured belongs to neither of them alone.

2One assumption ran through all of it

The sound went straight down and straight back

Every trace in the resolution modules was built for a wave arriving perpendicular to the boundary. That case has a name: normal incidence. It is the simplest one, the arithmetic is a single fraction, and it is where the impedance result comes from.

A real survey does not record that case, or rather it records it only once. A source is fired at one point and a line of receivers stretches away from it, so most of the energy that returns took a slanted path down and a slanted path back. The angle at which the wave meets the boundary is the incidence angle, and across a single recording it takes a range of values rather than one.

The resolution modules state this exclusion rather than hide it. Their trace module says it is normal incidence only, with no offset, no AVO and no mode conversion. The wedge module lists offset among the things left out because each deserves separate treatment. This set is that separate treatment.

Where the angles come from, how they relate to the distance between source and receiver, and how the traces are organized is the subject of module 04. What matters here is only that a range of angles exists and that the resolution modules covered one end of it.

3The reflection coefficient is not one number

The observation this set is about

Once the angle is allowed to vary, the reflection coefficient varies with it. The left-hand panel shows that for two rocks: a shale over a sand filled with brine, and the same shale over the same sand filled with gas. Both curves are computed from the full reflection equations rather than from the impedance fraction.

Two features of the picture are worth separating. The curves start at different heights, which is the impedance contrast from step 1 and is already familiar. They also have different shapes, and that is new: the gas curve falls away from its starting value much faster than the brine curve does.

The right-hand panel plots the gap between the two curves. It widens with angle. A measurement made only at normal incidence sees the difference at the left-hand edge of that plot; a measurement that uses the whole range sees the difference grow. That is the observation the rest of this set examines.

Producing those curves required a property of the rock that has not appeared in any module so far. Impedance describes how a rock responds to being compressed. A wave arriving at an angle also shears the rock, and how a rock resists shearing is a separate property that impedance does not carry. Where it comes from, and what controls it, is module 01.

WWhy it matters

What the extra angles are used for

Two measurements instead of one. A normal-incidence amplitude is a single number, and a single number cannot separate two unknowns. The variation with angle supplies a second number from the same recording, which is the basis of everything this set covers.

Fluid discrimination. The main practical use is distinguishing a sand filled with gas from the same sand filled with brine. The panels in step 3 show why that is possible in principle. How reliable it is in practice is what modules 05 through 08 examine.

What stacking costs. The image most interpreters work from is a stack, which averages the angle range into one trace. It is a cleaner picture and it discards the variation shown here. Both statements are true at once, and module 04 covers the trade.

The resolution limits still apply. Nothing in this set repeals tuning. An amplitude measured on a bed thinner than the resolution limit belongs to two merged reflections, and that is as true at 30° as at 0°.

Exercises

Each answer is a number read off this page at the settings described.

  1. Confirm that the impedance result still holds. Set the rock below to 2280 m/s and 1.91 g/cm³ with the rock above at 2700 m/s and 2.23 g/cm³, then find a different pair of numbers for the rock below that gives the same reflection coefficient.
    AnswerAny pair with the same product works, because the coefficient depends on velocity and density only through impedance. Roughly 2600 m/s at 1.67 g/cm³, or 1950 m/s at 2.23 g/cm³, land in the same place. This is the ambiguity the resolution modules already established, and it is the starting condition for this set rather than something this set fixes.
  2. Make the reflection disappear, then check what step 3 does. Match the impedances across the boundary so the trace goes flat, and note that step 3 is unaffected.
    AnswerSteps 1 and 2 use the four sliders; step 3 uses a modeled sand and shale instead. They are deliberately not connected. The sliders carry velocity and density, which is enough for a normal-incidence reflection and not enough for a reflection at an angle, and separating them is the cleanest way to show that.
  3. Read the two curves at their left-hand edge. With porosity at 30%, compare the brine and gas values at 0°.
    AnswerAbout −0.011 for brine and −0.162 for gas, a gap of roughly 0.151. At normal incidence the gas sand is a strong negative reflection and the brine sand is close to invisible. This much is impedance and would have been predictable from the resolution modules alone.
  4. Read them again at 40°. Move the angle slider to its maximum and compare the same two values.
    AnswerAbout −0.083 for brine and −0.282 for gas, a gap of roughly 0.199. Both became more negative, but the gas curve moved further, so the gap grew by about a third across the angle range. The extra angles did not just repeat the normal-incidence measurement more times; they carried information the normal incidence measurement did not have.
  5. Reduce the porosity and watch both curves. Take porosity from 35% down to 18% and follow the gap panel, then look at where the two curves start.
    AnswerThe gap shrinks at every angle: at normal incidence from about 0.172 to 0.100, and at 40° from about 0.212 to 0.176. A tight sand has little pore space, so its fluid has little influence on the rock as a whole. Something else changes at the same time — the brine sand's reflection coefficient at 0° crosses zero just below 30% porosity, because a tighter sand becomes harder than the shale above it rather than softer. The reflection has not stopped responding to the fluid; it has changed sign for a reason that has nothing to do with the fluid.

Key points

  • This set assumes the resolution modules. Impedance, the reflection coefficient, polarity, the wavelet and tuning are established there and used here without re-derivation.
  • Every one of those results assumed normal incidence — a wave meeting the boundary perpendicularly. It is the simplest case and the only one they covered.
  • A survey records a range of incidence angles, because the receivers are spread out from the source and most returning energy took a slanted path.
  • The reflection coefficient changes with angle, and two interfaces with different pore fluids change differently. That is the observation this set examines.
  • The variation supplies a second measurement from the same recording, which is what makes separating two unknowns possible in principle.
  • Describing the variation needs a property impedance does not carry — how the rock resists shearing rather than compression. Module 01 introduces it.

MMethod, simplifications and references

What is computed, and what is deferred

Steps 1 and 2 use the four sliders directly. Impedance is velocity times density, the reflection coefficient is the impedance difference over the sum, and the trace places a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet at the reflection. Nothing there needs a shear velocity, which is why the sliders do not carry one.

Step 3 does not use the sliders, apart from porosity. Its two rocks are a shale from Castagna's mudrock line and a sand built from a Hertz-Mindlin dry frame with the pore fluid inserted by Gassmann's relation, at 23 MPa and 64 °C, with brine and then with gas. The curves are the exact Zoeppritz P-wave reflection coefficients for those two rock pairs. Using the slider values there instead would require inventing a shear velocity for them, and the usual way of doing that is a shale trend that does not apply to sands.

Deferred rather than simplified:

  • Where the angles come from. The rays in step 2 are drawn straight and the angle is set directly. Real ray paths bend as velocity increases with depth, and the relationship between source-receiver distance and incidence angle is module 04.
  • Why the curves have the shape they do. Step 3 shows that they differ without explaining it. The explanation needs the shear response, which is modules 01 and 02, and the reflection equations themselves, which are module 05.
  • How well any of this survives real data. These curves are exact and noise-free. What happens to them once noise, a limited angle range and an unknown background are involved is modules 07 and 08.
  • Mode conversion, multiples, attenuation and anisotropy are all absent.

The porosity slider stops at 18%. The dry frame here is a soft-sand Hertz-Mindlin model, which describes a grain pack held together by contact stiffness and is not intended for well-cemented rock. Run below roughly 18% and it keeps returning numbers, but they describe a grain pack that no longer resembles the tight sand being asked about, and the difference between the two fluid cases at wide angle stops behaving monotonically for that reason rather than a physical one. A cemented-sand or constant-cement model would be the appropriate choice there.

The angle range stops at 40°, which is a common practical limit for usable angles on a marine gather at reservoir depth. Beyond roughly that angle the reflection coefficient starts to change rapidly, the approximations most people use stop holding, and the processing that flattened the gather is less reliable.
