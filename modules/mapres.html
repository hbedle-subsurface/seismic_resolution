Where does my channel really end?

Module 04 shrank two points down until they became one. Here is the same limit acting on something you would actually map: a channel that narrows downstream and splits into two distributaries. Watch the point where it appears to stop, and the point where the two branches stop looking like one. Neither of those places is in the geology — they are in the acquisition.

Try this

  1. Look at what you are given before touching anything. The page opens on unmigrated data, which is where every survey starts. The channel is a broad smudge. You could tell somebody a channel runs through here and roughly which way, and almost nothing else — not where it ends, not that it forks at all. Now press 3D. That one step is the difference between a smudge and a map, and it is worth remembering that this is what the data looked like before somebody did it for you.
  2. With 3D on, compare the two maps. Where does the channel stop in each? The imaged channel fades out well before the real one does. Everything downstream of that point is still sand, still the same thickness, and completely absent from your map. If you were mapping the extent of a reservoir from this, you would draw the boundary in the wrong place and never know.
  3. Find where the branches become two. Drag the section line upstream from the far end and watch the profile. Far downstream the two branches sit well apart and read as two. Move upstream and they close until the trough between them fills in, and from there back to the split they image as one wide channel. The map shows a single channel that forks once, when in fact it forks much earlier.
  4. Take it deeper. Move the channel from 1.2 s to 3 s. Resolution worsens and the apparent end of the channel marches upstream. The same body, unchanged, gets shorter on the map as it gets deeper. Comparing feature sizes between a shallow field and a deep one is not comparing like with like.
  5. Try to fix it with frequency. Push the peak frequency up and watch the apparent end move. It helps, and it is the only thing here that helps, but the improvement is proportional to frequency while the Fresnel zone shrinks only as its square root. Bandwidth is expensive and the returns are steady rather than dramatic.
  6. Starve the aperture. Pull the aperture down to 500 m. The image degrades toward the unmigrated case. If your target sits near the edge of the survey, the migration has only one side to gather from, and features that are perfectly resolved in the middle of the survey quietly disappear at the margin.
  7. Switch to 2D. Resolution is good along the line and poor across it, so the channel smears in one direction only. Whether you can see the branches now depends on which way they happen to trend relative to your line — a geological conclusion decided by the survey orientation.

What to carry away

  • Features narrower than the resolution do not go blurry, they go missing. A narrowing channel appears to end, and the ending looks convincing.
  • Two things closer together than the resolution image as one thing. Branches, stacked channels, closely spaced faults — all merge.
  • The limit is roughly λ/4 after good 3D migration, and the width of the Fresnel zone without it, which is often ten times worse.
  • Boundaries you map are partly a property of the survey. Reprocess with more bandwidth or a wider aperture and the channel gets longer, with no new rock involved.
  • Absence of evidence is weak evidence here. "The channel ends" and "the channel narrows below what we can see" look identical on a map.
Method, assumptions, and where to read more

The channel is drawn as a map of amplitude: uniform inside the body, zero outside, so that thickness and tuning are held out of it and only horizontal resolution is on trial. Module 07 does the opposite and holds width fixed while thickness varies.

Imaging is done as a low-pass filter in the wavenumber domain, using the two-dimensional FFT: spatial detail finer than the resolution length is removed, everything coarser is kept, with a raised-cosine taper across the cutoff. That is what a band-limited image is. The cutoff is calibrated so that two features separated by exactly the stated resolution are on the point of separating — checked against the model across a factor of eight in scale, where the split occurs between 0.96 and 1.02 times the quoted number.

The resolution length itself is the same as module 04: the Fresnel zone radius (V/2)·√(t/f) without migration, and λ/(4 sin θ) with it, where θ is the largest angle the aperture allows. In 2D the filter is applied along the line only, so the cross-line direction keeps the Fresnel limit.

Left out: noise, tuning, velocity error, dip, migration artifacts, and the fact that a real channel has a fill whose properties change along it. Every one of them makes the edge harder to find, not easier.
